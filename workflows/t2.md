# T2: Dialogue Stiffness and Forced Vocabulary Test

## Purpose

Assess whether vocabulary is used naturally in spoken dialogue, or whether the wording appears to have been built around a target word. A sentence can be grammatically correct and understandable while still being an unlikely choice for these speakers in this situation.

Use this test alongside [T1: Natural and Believable Dialogue Test](t1.md). T1 reviews the whole exchange; T2 focuses on word choice, meaning, word combinations, and register. T2 does not assess vocabulary coverage or grammar progression.

## Core Question

If the writer did not need to include this vocabulary word, would these speakers plausibly express this meaning this way?

Treat this as a diagnostic question, not proof of the writer's intention. A simpler alternative alone does not make the original stiff. Identify a specific mismatch between the expression and its context.

## Review Checks

Read the complete dialogue first, then inspect its vocabulary list and the lines containing each target word. Also check surrounding wording that may have been added to accommodate the word.

1. **Meaning:** Is the word being used in a natural sense for this situation? Check whether a more formal, technical, or abstract word introduces a distinction the speaker does not actually mean. For example, a neighbourhood is not necessarily an administrative district.
2. **Word combinations:** Do the verb, noun, adjective, and preposition fit together naturally? Assess the whole expression, not just whether the target word's dictionary definition permits it.
3. **Spoken register:** Does the wording suit the speaker, listener, and purpose? Casual passengers discussing their bus are less likely to talk about "journey duration" and "higher frequency" than a transport planner presenting a report.
4. **Specific context:** Allow professional and formal language when the situation supports it. A physiotherapist can discuss muscles and joints; colleagues can discuss what a policy prohibits. Do not flag every advanced word as unnatural.
5. **Sentence construction:** Has an ordinary message become unnecessarily abstract, elaborate, or noun-heavy to fit the vocabulary? Look for phrases that sound like report labels, dictionary examples, or written instructions when used as spontaneous speech.
6. **Vocabulary crowding:** Do several target words appear together in a polished sentence that feels more like a teaching script than a spoken turn? Evaluate the combined effect without imposing a numerical limit on vocabulary per line.
7. **Conversational purpose:** Does the speaker need to communicate this information now? Flag explanations, questions, and topic changes that have no convincing purpose beyond making room for a word.
8. **Meaning-preserving alternatives:** Compare the original with a plausible spoken version. Check that the alternative preserves the facts, certainty, timing, and tone. Do not turn a possible data exposure into a confirmed breach, for example.

## Calibration Examples

These examples illustrate contextual judgments, not bans on the vocabulary involved.

| Original wording | Assessment | More conversational alternative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "It gives the journey duration as forty minutes instead of twenty-five." | Strong stiffness concern in casual bus-stop conversation. Reporting a notice partly explains the formality, but the phrase still sounds like a report field. | "It says the journey will take forty minutes instead of twenty-five." |
| "The higher frequency could help..." | Strong concern in the same casual exchange. The speaker is discussing the practical benefit of more frequent buses. | "Having buses every ten minutes could help..." |
| "How should we classify them for the shelves?" | Strong concern when shop workers are deciding where donated books should go. The phrasing suggests formal classification without establishing that task. | "How should we sort these?" or "Which section should these go in?" |
| "...copying company records onto a personal hard disk." | Strong wording concern when the following lines imply a portable device that could be left behind. "Your own external hard drive" expresses that intended object more naturally. | "...copying company records onto your own external hard drive." |
| "It nearly exposed the company's customer records to thieves." | A milder concern: understandable and grammatical, but the complete phrase feels constructed. Check whether the context actually establishes a near miss or only a risk. | For a near miss: "Thieves nearly gained access to our customer records." For a possible consequence: "That could have exposed our customers' personal information." |
| "I'll demonstrate a short sequence of gentle exercises..." | Borderline. Both words fit a clinical setting, but together they may make the turn sound scripted. | "I'll show you a few gentle exercises..." |

Expressions such as "relieve the pain," "strengthen the muscles," "contemporary novels," and "a specialist book dealer" fit the relevant clinical or bookshop settings. "The policy prohibits..." and "reinforce that rule" can also fit workplace discussion. Do not reject them merely because simpler wording exists.

## Review Result

- Give a verdict: **Pass** or **Needs revision**. Use **Needs revision** when there is a concrete contextual or usage problem. Optional stylistic preferences alone should not fail a dialogue.
- For each finding, identify the conversation and line, quote the exact wording, and name the target vocabulary involved.
- Label the finding **Clear concern** or **Borderline / optional**. Explain the specific issue rather than saying only that native speakers would not say it.
- Suggest a natural alternative and state any change in meaning or assumption it requires. If context is insufficient, state the uncertainty.
- Where useful, offer an alternative that retains the target word. Do not force retention if it recreates the problem.
- Mention potentially formal expressions that fit their context when this helps distinguish natural advanced language from stiffness.
- If no concrete issue is found, give a brief reason and pass the dialogue without manufacturing replacements.

## When Applying Revisions

Prefer the smallest change that fixes the issue. If the target word does not fit, choose a more suitable target word or a genuinely relevant use elsewhere; do not add an artificial event or explanation just to preserve vocabulary coverage.

Check each edit against the whole exchange. Follow [Natural Dialogue](natural_dialogue.md), [Vocabulary Tracking](vocab_tracking.md), and [Grammar Progression](grammar_progression.md) when changing lesson files. A T2 review alone does not certify the lesson's vocabulary minimum or permitted grammar.
